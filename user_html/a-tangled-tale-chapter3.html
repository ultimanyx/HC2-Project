Chapter 3

Knot III - Mad Mathesis

"You are a great mathematician, I believe?" said the young lady, looking up at the professor.

"I am fond of the science," he replied, modestly.

"Then, perhaps, you can tell me the answer to this problem," said the young lady. "Two trains start at the same time from two stations, which are a hundred miles apart. One train travels at the rate of thirty miles an hour, and the other at twenty miles an hour. A fly starts from the front of one train and flies to the front of the other, and then back again, and so on, until the trains meet. If the fly travels at the rate of sixty miles an hour, how far will it have travelled?"

"That is a very old problem," said the professor. "It is generally attributed to a French mathematician. The answer is simple. The trains are approaching each other at the rate of fifty miles an hour. They are a hundred miles apart, so they will meet in two hours. The fly travels at the rate of sixty miles an hour, so in two hours it will have travelled a hundred and twenty miles."

"But how do you know it flies at that rate?" asked the young lady.

"Because," said the professor, "the problem states it."

"Ah," said the young lady, "but the problem doesn't state it. It only says the fly flies at the rate of sixty miles an hour. It doesn't say it flies in a straight line. It might fly round in circles."

"That is true," said the professor. "But in that case the problem is impossible to solve, for we do not know the length of the circles."

"Then the problem is a bad one," said the young lady.

"Most problems are," said the professor, with a sigh.
